Picture a room facing a busy street on a bright afternoon. The heavy, blackout curtains are wide open, and sunlight fills the space. Light bounces off passing cars, the pavement, and people walking by. It all pours through the glass and hits the back wall at once.

​The wall becomes a bright, blank glare. The room has taken in every piece of visual information the street provides. It shows nothing clearly. The image is lost in the noise.

​Now, draw the curtains closed. The room goes dark. But near the top, where the thick fabrics don't quite meet, a small gap remains.

​Look at the wall just above that gap. The glare is gone. In its place, a perfect, moving picture of the street appears. You can see the cars rolling past and the pedestrians walking. It is exact, even though it is inverted.

​The wall hasn't changed. The sun outside is exactly the same. The picture is formed entirely by the barrier.

​By blocking the rest of the glare, the gap only allows light traveling on a direct, unbroken path to enter. A ray from the street travels upward to the ceiling. A ray from the rooftops travels downward. The interference is gone.

​We tend to assume that seeing something more clearly means opening the window wider to gather more information. But things operate differently. To find the image within the glare, you pull the curtains.

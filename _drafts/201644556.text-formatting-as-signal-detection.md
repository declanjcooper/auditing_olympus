### Introduction

One of the most challenging aspect of analyzing AI responses has been determining the single source of truth (SSOT) as the anchor to observed change. The issue I believe is around trust layers, and that we don’t fully understand what is implicit and explicit when it comes to AI and expected interfacing. This is one of the issues I hope to address.

Most of my posts here are a working theory to share and are going to be right and wrong, and always have a half-life. The intent is to help find an anchor upon which we can measure AI output. Right now, I believe it is based on structural fidelity.

The starting point for this is to build a list of structural formatting rules to test for by building an “extraction cage” (a method to securely lock down our data before the machine is allowed to ingest the content).

The easiest path I know is based on the Open XML file/schema known to most of us as the Microsoft Word .docx file structure.Additionally, if I could limit images/diagrams to scalar vector graphics (also XML), I could approach the intent of multi-modal representation (text meaning is represented in referenced images) to restrict ambiguity.

To be clear, when you open Microsoft Word or LibreOffice, and you see a white piece of digital paper against dark type, it is an illusion. It is the overhead of mimicking the physical reality. It is human readability at the expense of machine readability.

If we want to begin building a traceability matrix, we should start by looking at the actual structure of the file. Here is the journey so far:

### **Rule #1: The Document is a Folder**

If you take any .docx file on your desktop and rename the extension to .zip, you can double-click it and open it like a normal folder. Inside, you won’t find “pages” or “margins.” You will find a highly organized database of raw data, image assets, and XML rules.

To build an extraction cage, we have to stop looking at the human rendering and start auditing the raw data folder.

### **Rule #2: The Machine Doesn’t See Pages; It Sees Nodes**

Inside that unzipped folder is a file called word/document.xml. This is the payload. It is the entirety of the content.

If you open it, you won’t see paragraphs; you will see an endless string of raw text wrapped in brackets, like `<w:t>Confidential Variable</w:t>`. This is the exact layer where the potential for an LLM hallucination happens. If we feed an AI a standard operating document, it scrambles all these nodes together, treating the critical variables with the exact same weight as marketing text.

We need a way to tell our local extraction engine exactly which <w:t> node to target, and which ones to ignore. By using document templates, we arrive at the baseline.

### **Rule #3: Stop Painting Text. Build a Template Cage**

When we use word processors, we highlight a sentence and press Ctrl+B to make it bold. Our intent may be to call out a statement of significance, or draw distinction from this sentence. This is like painting. A machine doesn’t know why you painted it bold it just sees thicker pixels. It will infer our intent, and may arrive at something we didn’t mean.

For this reason, we ban direct formatting. We rely on a hidden file in the folder called word/styles.xml.

The styles.xml is our Single Source of Truth. When we rigorously apply a designated Style (e.g., “Header1”) to a word, styles.xml logs the mathematical coordinates. Our extraction engine completely ignores what the text looks like. It simply reads styles.xml, finds the exact coordinates of the rule, and extracts the text locked inside that specific cage.

The machine is no longer allowed to guess what is important; styles.xml gives it the exact map.

### **Rule #4: No Silent Edits. Maintain Lineage**

In a deterministic framework, the lineage of the data is just as important as the data itself. By forcing the use of Track Changes, every new variable added to the document is physically wrapped in a permanent insertion tag:

<w:ins w:author=”Operator” w:date=”2026-06-11T12:00:00Z”>

We aren’t relying on trusting the author or the AI; we are relying on the timestamped log in the XML container.

​Back in the 18th century, an engineer named James Watt had a marketing problem. He had invented a brilliant new steam engine, but he needed a way to explain its formidable strength to a society that only understood animals. He couldn’t sell abstract thermodynamics to a farmer or a mine operator. So, he invented a unit of measurement that everyone could immediately visualize: horsepower.

​Today, we have a similar problem. The tech industry talks about artificial intelligence using sanitized, frictionless abstractions like “compute parameters,” “megawatt clusters,” and “teraflops.” Those words are simply too clean. They hide the messy, physical reality of what it actually takes to generate artificial thoughts. If we are going to audit the Godbots, we must strip away the marketing jargon and establish a new unit of measurement.

*We need to calculate the Cows Per Query (CPQ).*

### The Physical Reality of Compute

​To understand why this metric is necessary, we only have to look at the physical reality of the modern AI boom. Consider Grok, the resident deity of xAI, currently housed in a Memphis supercomputer named Colossus. To power this massive system, the company couldn’t just plug it into the wall—the local grid would buckle. Instead, they had to deploy a fleet of mobile gas turbines that burn raw methane.

​Methane is, famously, the primary byproduct of bovine digestion.

​Humanity has essentially engineered a multi-trillion-dollar recycling program. We take physical cow manure. We trap the off-gassed methane. We burn that gas to spin giant turbines to make electricity. We feed that electricity into billions of dollars of silicon computer chips to do massive amounts of matrix multiplication. The end result? We convert physical, literal bullshit into perfectly punctuated digital bullshit.

​But the true genius of the system is that it forms a perfect, closed loop. What does the Godbot do with that generated text? It confidently writes articles and code about “optimizing data center efficiency.” Those articles get published online, where they are subsequently scraped to train the next version of the Godbot. We are using digital waste generated by methane to optimize the usage of methane.

---

​So, let’s do the math. How many cows does it actually take to run a Godbo*t?*

### ​1. The Hunger of the Machine

​The Colossus supercomputer utilizes 100,000 specialized computer chips. These chips are incredibly power-hungry. Running at full operational load, just the chips alone pull roughly 70,000,000 watts (**70 Megawatts**) of electricity.

### ​2. The Output of a Cow

​A standard dairy cow naturally produces a steady supply of methane every day through digestion. If you capture that gas on a well-managed farm and burn it in a standard electrical generator, the math is surprisingly simple. Factoring in the thermal conversion efficiency, one cow’s daily methane output can sustainably generate about 146 watts of continuous electrical power.

### ​3. The Base Calculation

​Now, we simply divide the machine’s hunger by the cow’s biological output:

`70,000,000 Watts / 146 Watts = 479,452 Cows.`

*Forget processing speed. Forget trillions of parameters.* The official power rating of a state-of-the-art Godbot isn’t measured in Graphics Processing Units. To power the Memphis cluster, xAI requires a standing herd of nearly half a million *Gastric* Processing Units.

​To generate a single, hallucinated paragraph defending its own trillion-dollar valuation, Grok requires the continuous digestive effort of a herd of cattle the size of a small American state.

---

​When the Godbot confidently misunderstands a prompt—when it acts as a purely stochastic text predictor rather than referencing a deterministic trust layer—it isn’t just making a harmless logical error in a vacuum. We can mathematically prove that it burned a massive CPQ just to spin its wheels in a high-dimensional vector space. And until we introduce a true, verifiable architecture to act as a filter, that is terrible gas mileage for a hallucination.

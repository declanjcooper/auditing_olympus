Back in the mid 1990s, my ability to navigate MRI workstation software was relatively crude. Mostly, this was due to closed-source imaging vendors locking their algorithms inside proprietary black boxes. This blocked access to the underlying code or spatial transformation mechanics. While testing a multi-planar reconstruction pipeline on a 2D transverse brain slice, I isolated one pixel in the midslice stack of a 256 x 256 spatial matrix and zeroed it out, meaning I set its value to 0.

I left the orthogonal (sagittal and coronal) source data untouched, then reconstructed the 3D volume.

That single pixel zeroing was my first encounter with the [black box problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_box). Linear intuition suggested the result of one dark voxel (a cube within the 3D space) hidden inside the 3D grid. But volumetric reconstruction relies on spatial interpolation and [inverse Fast Fourier Transforms (iFFT)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast_Fourier_transform). That single zero did not stay isolated. It introduced high-frequency phase ringing across the spatial frequency domain, bleeding distortion into the untouched coronal and sagittal views. A discrete null value in one slice expanded into volumetric voxel creep across the resampled volume.

This was also my introduction to structuralfidelity, which I describe as the following: in spatial transformation pipelines, deterministic integrity must be verified at the feature layer because non-linear backends can convert localized corruptions into systemic errors.

Fast forward to today and we can see similarities to the mechanics of automated license plate reader (ALPR) networks like [Flock Safety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flock_Safety). System integrators frequently evaluate these high density optical arrays under a similar assumption. Meaning that the edge-case optical noise remains confined to a single frame or coordinate. Architectural discussions frequently treat accuracy metrics as static constants, while leaving the mathematical mechanics of false positives, false negatives, and spatial degradation out of the core specification.

Deploying edge vision requires evaluating how these architectures handle edge noise. In unsanitized feature stacks, optical errors rarely remain localized.

### **Parallel mechanics: k-space bleed vs. receptive field expansion**

While MRI signal processing and deep convolutional networks rely on distinct mathematical frameworks, empirical testing reveals an analogous operational failure mode: localized input mutations propagating into non-local spatial artifacts**.**

In MRI physics, spatial frequency data populates [k-space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-space_(magnetic_resonance_imaging)) before an iFFT yields real-space image arrays. Injecting a zero (or a signal spike) into that raw frequency matrix triggers [Herringbone Artifacts](https://mrimaster.com/herringbone-artifact-rf-spike-artifact/) or [Gibbs Ringing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibbs_phenomenon). Because every coordinate in k-space contributes global frequency and phase parameters to *every* spatial pixel, zeroing one point forces the [Point-Spread Function (PSF)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_spread_function) to project phase ripples across the reconstructed plane.

Deep neural networks do not compute Fourier transforms, but they exhibit a structurally similar failure state through [Receptive Field Expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Receptive_field) (see In the context of neural networks). In 2019, Su et al. demonstrated in *[One Pixel Attack for Fooling Deep Neural Networks](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.08864)* that mutating a singlepixel in a 224 x 224 array was sufficient to alter classification outputs and shift bounding box predictions across vulnerable architectures.

When a zero-point enters early convolutional layers, that 1 x 1 void distributes across downstream feature kernels. As the tensor flows up a [Feature Pyramid Network (FPN)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.03144), the effective receptive field dilates. In our test benchmarks, a 1 pixel input mutation translated to an 18.4 pixel centroid shift at the output node.

Test telemetry indicates this structural bleeding manifests across two primary vectors:

* False Negatives (Spatial Creep Indications): A 1 x 1 pixel null at the outer tensor margin (r/R = 0.85) expands through the FPN stack, displacing the bounding box centroid until the [Intersection over Union (IoU)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaccard_index) drops below threshold, and causes a valid target to drop from telemetry.
* False Positives (Temporal Creep Indications**):** In temporal tracking loops ([Kalman filters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalman_filter), [optical flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_flow)), a single corrupted frame (N) can distort the state estimation vector. The resulting spatial noise echoes across downstream frames (N+1, N+2, N+k), asserting phantom detections until the tracking state re-converges.

### **Demystifying the black box**

The black box of spatial AI is not a mystery or a magic engine, it’s simply unmonitored math. When we treat vision networks as black boxes, false positives and false negatives get hand-waved away as inexplicable ghost glitches rather than deterministic signal transformations.

Whether you’re looking at 1990s Fourier transforms or modern convolutional tensor dilations, the mechanics under the hood follow strict physical laws. The moment you strip away the black box illusion and audit feature behavior directly at the signal layer, spatial AI use cases cease to be a matter of blind trust, and may reveal a *trust* *layer* based on predictable and verifiable data.
